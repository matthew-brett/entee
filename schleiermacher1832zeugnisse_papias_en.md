It has always grieved me, whenever I found these testimonies treated with a certain contempt. For even granting that the praise of the man which we now read in a passage of Eusebius[^735a] is an interpolation—as I will not deny—and that this historian formed only the later unfavourable judgement of him from inspection of his writing[^735b]: he may still have been of limited mind, yet his testimonies seem to me to lose nothing thereby, since here little turns upon his judgement. And how great a desire he had to gather oral information, as also that he was in a position to question such men as had themselves heard the apostles of the Lord—this he himself relates to us in so naïve and
<!--- page 736 -->
unprejudiced a manner[^736a] that we can harbour as little suspicion against this statement as Eusebius himself. But however unimpeachable these testimonies appear to me: so little do I see in them what people commonly infer from them, when they still always cite Papias as an authority for the claim that our Gospel of Matthew was originally written in Hebrew. To be sure, he says that Matthew wrote in Hebrew: but does he also say that this Hebrew writing was translated by others, and does he also mean our Gospel of Matthew? The one of these, I should think, is very much open to doubt; the other is to be denied most definitely. Indeed, it is scarcely conceivable how anyone could have hit upon the idea of referring Papias’s expressions—which Eusebius here plainly quotes verbatim—literally to our Gospel.[^736b] The error is indeed very old; I should even maintain that Eusebius too had no other ground for this assumption.[^736c] But whence? Surely not because τὰ λόγια συνεγράψατο could mean as much as: he compiled the narratives of Christ’s deeds, or he described his life! Our Papias’s own and only work was, as is well known, styled λογίων κυριακῶν ἐξηγήσεις,[^736d] and
<!--- page 737 -->
Jerome[^737a] translates it most correctly as *sermonum Domini explanatio*. Would he then have been able to render τὰ λόγια συνεγράψατο otherwise than as *sermones Domini conscripsit*? Then indeed he too, believing that the Gospel of Matthew still extant in Hebrew in his day was the original, would have had to assume that Matthew, besides the Gospel—hence probably earlier—had written a collection of sayings of Christ. This he does not in fact do. But since he knew Papias’s books and thus this passage also:[^737b] he as well as Eusebius, and behind them all who assumed a Hebrew original of our Matthew, reasoned as follows. Because we have a Gospel κατὰ Ματθαῖον, and do not already know from elsewhere that Matthew wrote something else: Papias can have meant nothing else than our Gospel. As if it stood so rock-firm that κατὰ Ματθαῖον can mean nothing other than “composed by Matthew”! And as if learning from Papias that Matthew wrote something else were not just as good as already knowing it from elsewhere! Certainly, in any other field such a conclusion would long since have been disallowed! And those who do not assume a Hebrew original of our Gospel of Matthew? They seem not to have thought it worth the trouble to look and see whether Papias perhaps says something else, but have overlooked the σμικρὸς τὸν νοῦν, as
<!--- page 738 -->
if for that reason he must always have nothing to say! But here it is not at all a matter of his judgement, but of a simple fact; he knew the book, for he knew what others had further done with it; he had the report concerning the author from the best hands, and his report therefore deserves that we should do it full justice. Then, however, we must—provisionally unconcerned with our Gospel, which here remains wholly out of play, and concerning which we in any case have no such early reports—say after him: Matthew wrote a collection of sayings of Christ, whether these were individual sayings, or longer discourses, or both, as is most probable. For Papias’s expression simply cannot mean anything else. The word λόγιον is everywhere, where it occurs, to be explained from its ordinary meaning “oracle of a god,” to which the word has increasingly been restricted. Thus the LXX use it of the individual commandments God gave, of all divine utterances, whether made known through whatever theophanies or through a prophetic mouth; only a few times is something from which one believes one can extract a divine oracle also called λόγιον. With the same right those who use the Bible for looking up sayings as decisions may call it their λόγιον; but how could one in this sense say of the authors τὰ λόγια συνεγράψαντο! In the N.T. the word occurs twice of the Law of Moses,[^738a] precisely because every individual commandment of it was a divine oracle. Of Christian things it is said once[^738b]
<!--- page 739 -->
quite generally, that what the Christian speaks should be as oracles of God. More specifically of the revelation of God given us in Christ, when it is said[^739a] that the Hebrews still needed to be taught the elements of the beginning of the oracles of God; for the whole phrase is afterwards explained[^739b] of the first fundamental truths of Christianity. In this sense, then, one could indeed say of someone who had written a compendium of the revealed doctrines τὰ λόγια συνεγράψατο, the more he had remained true to the original form. So too those first teachings, through the presentation of which repentance and faith are first to be elicited, are called μαιευτικὰ λόγια;[^739c] and naturally, once the canon already stood quite firm and these books were regarded as containing all the oracles of God, the collection of the holy Scriptures itself could be called τὰ λόγια.[^739d] But nothing of all this leads to the composition of a Gospel-book being so designated. One single passage has struck me where it might seem so, namely where Photius[^739e] says of Ephraim: αἱ δὲ γραφαί εἰσιν αὐτῷ ἥ τε παλαιὰ διαθήκη καὶ τὰ κυριακὰ λόγια, καὶ τὰ ἀποστολικὰ κηρύγματα. Yet plainly here too λόγια mean the Gospels as little as κηρύγματα mean the Epistles; rather Ephraim was to furnish the Persian to whom his letter is addressed with the proof of the Trinity
<!--- page 740 -->
from Scripture. Now this proof could be drawn from the Epistles only insofar as they contained the general teaching of the apostles, their κήρυγμα; all other discussions occurring in this epistle could contribute nothing to it. So too the narratives in the Gospels could not be used for it, but chiefly only the sayings of Christ. Hence the κυριακὰ λόγια here are the same as in Papias; and this whole course leads us back only to the foregoing as the sole possible sense: Matthew wrote down the sayings of Christ. For that Papias here does not also say κυριακά, but λόγια simply, can hardly mislead anyone. Later one could also call teachings, instructions, and other utterances of the apostles λόγια, insofar as the Holy Spirit spoke through them; but Matthew could not yet compile such, and New Testament λόγια—and they were surely nothing else that he collected—could only be sayings of Christ.
If we now take this as established according to our author, and ask ourselves whether it is at all probable that the same Matthew later, after this collection of discourses, still wrote our Gospel: I can only answer with a complete negative. For certainly in such a writing embracing the whole life of Christ, in which he is careful nowhere to refer to that collection, he would also have had to take up the former discourses again, even granting that he added others, since he will first have recorded those that appeared to him the most significant. But how incredible that this second writing should have remained unknown to Papias! How still more incredible that he should not then have remarked with a few words in this very passage: Later, however, he also added the historical matter together with a
<!--- page 741 -->
few other discourses! And no less incredible, surely, that Eusebius should have grudged us this addition, mentioning the book that did not become New Testament while passing over the first New Testament one, since he plainly communicates the whole passage only in order—as is his manner—to show what New Testament books were already known to Papias. No: Eusebius found in his Papias nothing of two writings of Matthew; hence only this one is likely to have existed, and that was not our Gospel. So much follows easily and surely enough; but how that writing of Matthew has so utterly disappeared, and in what way our Gospel[^741a] could have appropriated precisely the name of Matthew—these are the difficult, almost insoluble questions that press upon us together with that certainty, unfortunately as inescapable as they are difficult. Concerning the first our author does tell us something further, which however seems little suited to bring us nearer our goal. He tells us namely: As Matthew compiled the discourses of Christ in writing, so afterwards each, as well as he was able—yes, what? translated them, people say generally! The words[^741a] can indeed mean that; but the more people translated them, probably all into the same language, Greek, the less can they have been lost. And yet of all these translations neither the least trace is found among the next later church writers, nor did it fall to Eusebius or Jerome to get sight of any of them! And must ἡρμήνευσε necessarily mean “translate”? The word has a very wide range, and certainly means “translate” only where two
<!--- page 742 -->
languages were already under discussion, or where the whole context necessarily requires it. But neither the one nor the other is the case here. Plainly by the structure of the sentence ἡρμήνευσε is set not in connection with τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ, but with συνεγράψατο. Matthew compiled the discourses, and indeed in the Hebrew language, and others have them—here “translated” is by no means necessarily required. If then it causes great difficulty, because we do not know how we are to dispose of all these translations: why do we not rather say, Others have, as each was able, explained, elucidated, applied them? Thereby we escape great difficulty. For of so many translations at least one would surely have been preserved long enough for the work still to remain known and in circulation later; but through elaborations that incorporated its substance it could most easily perish, while these remained. Our author also calls his elaborations ἑρμηνείας,[^742a] and how useful it could be to us if more of his work were extant, so that we might know how much or how little he comprised under this expression, and thus keep our attempts at explanation within his usage! Still, something remains from which we can see that he takes the word in a very wide sense. Andrew of Caesarea[^742b]
<!--- page 743 -->
cites from him, that to some angels it was also entrusted to rule over the ordering and administration of the earth. To whichever of Christ’s known utterances about the angels one may refer this, none of them is thereby elucidated any more closely; indeed one can scarcely think that such an utterance of Christ’s should have been in circulation. One can therefore only say that Papias used the occasion offered him by an utterance of Christ to introduce this notice. Irenaeus[^743a] likewise cites the description of a gigantic vine in the kingdom of God. This belongs clearly enough to sayings of Christ such as we read in Matt. 26:29, Mark 14:25, Luke 22:18. These so very uniform formulations scarcely suggest that others were still in circulation; but even if so, the Saviour’s words are certainly not set in a clearer light by this description. Most fully, however, we have a narrative of the end of Judas wholly divergent from our New Testament one.[^743b] This, since Papias
<!--- page 744 -->
commented only on discourses of the Lord, not on Gospel narratives, he can only have presented in connection with such utterances as Matt. 26:24 and Luke 22:21–22. But this saying too is not further clarified, whether Judas died in this way or that. If then Papias reckons such descriptions and narratives, which stand in some connection with a saying of Christ, as belonging to the ἑρμηνεία of it: what wholly diverse uses and elaborations of Matthew’s collection, whether composed in Hebrew or in Greek, may then not be comprised under that formula ἡρμήνευσε δ᾽ αὐτὰ ὡς ἠδύνατο ἕκαστος? Uses of individual parts, when they were unfolded homiletically and applied to given circumstances; elaborations, partly purely explanatory, when someone sought as far as possible to resolve the difficulties that present themselves in understanding, partly so mixed and variegated by manifold digressions as Papias’s own work would also have been, if he had taken Matthew’s collection as his basis. But were not also, according to his usage, such works among the ἑρμηνεῖαι as added to the discourses and utterances of Christ the local and temporal circumstances
<!--- page 745 -->
under which they were spoken? For that plainly contributes far more to understanding, is far more hermeneutical, than those incidental digressive narratives!

And these labours of others upon Matthew’s collection seem not to have been wholly lost to us; indeed they have probably preserved the collection itself for us as well. For such a labour is our Gospel of Matthew; looked at more closely it stands to the apostolic collection of discourses precisely as I have just described. It includes this collection within itself, and adds the local and temporal circumstances, at places where they could not be wanting, from the oral tradition of the original and proper evangelists; often, however, also in such a way that one notices the elaborator’s uncertainty. Then he has only general localities—the mountain, the lake—and general temporalities, such as must always have recurred: Christ goes about in the cities and teaches in the synagogues, or he travels and the people gather about him, he suffers under the press, he heals and works miracles, or he returns to the city where he has his usual dwelling. My intention here is by no means to give an exact analysis of the Gospel, nor yet to compare this view of it on all sides with the prevailing one, or to bring back to this the doubts already several times raised against the apostolic origin of this Gospel, but only provisionally to set up these two points: first, that we have no reason to deny what so ancient and for this purpose so well qualified a reporter tells us, that the apostle Matthew composed in the Palestinian dialect a compilation of discourses and sayings of Christ, upon which afterwards many others worked, each in his own way; and second, that our Gospel of Matthew
<!--- page 746 -->
is such a labour, and precisely for that reason bears the name κατὰ Ματθαῖον, because it rests upon that writing of Matthew. I already take the first point to be sufficiently established, since anyone who would explain Papias’s words otherwise does the greatest violence to the language. The second I should like to make somewhat clearer still, in order to direct the attention of the competent to it; but for now I can only try, by way of sample, to separate the fundamental writing from the present book, and here and there make noticeable how well or how poorly the later labour joins on.
Let us for the present leave aside all that precedes, and begin where we can first look for λόγια, namely where Christ appears publicly. Here then the first section of the collection meets us in chs. 5–7. I call the Sermon on the Mount a gnomology, without wishing to deny that larger pieces belong together, but quite certain that it was not spoken by Christ thus as one whole. The appearance of its unity is to be put to the account of him who fashioned the historical frame about the first work; this appearance arises because he has Christ, after such a general presentation as we read in ch. 4:23–25, go up a mountain—which we have only to seek somewhere in the whole Galilean land—and, where this probably first section of the collection ended, come down again from the mountain to go to Capernaum. This appearance is indeed further strengthened by the formula 7:28, Καὶ ἐγένετο ὅτε ἐτέλεσεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς τοὺς λόγους τούτους. Yet this will mislead no one who has once grasped the right point of view: we find similar ones where Christ has just as little spoken consecutively what is thus framed. A second section of the collection is ch. 10, instructions for the apostles. For these too it follows from the formula ch. 11:1, Καὶ ἐγένετο ὅτε ἐτέλεσεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς διατάσσων κ.τ.λ.,
<!--- page 747 -->
that this was not spoken consecutively as one discourse. One may hope that already here, the second time, the formula will be familiar to everyone as the close of a section of the discourse-collection and a transition to other matter. The introduction here naturally had to give the list of the apostles’ names and recount their authenticating prerogatives; but the word immediately preceding[^747a] stands in so close a connection with this equipping of the apostles that I do not doubt this verse too stood at the head of the following instructions in the apostle’s collection, and hence I already regard 9:35–36 as our evangelist’s usual introduction to this section. A third section from Matthew’s work is the collection of parables, ch. 13:1–52. That these cannot have been spoken thus one after another lies not only in the nature of the case, but can also be shown most clearly from the narrative itself. Yet they stood with good right, because of their similarity, side by side in the λόγια, and for that reason they are framed just so: in front the general locality, the lake and the so frequent state of crowding, and at the close v. 53 the usual Καὶ ἐγένετο ὅτε ἐτέλεσεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς τὰς παραβολὰς ταύτας and the general locality μετῆρεν ἐκεῖθεν; but here, as I have already remarked elsewhere, one cannot even know where the ἐκεῖθεν is. If I now leap to ch. 18: I by no means thereby assert that in what lies between there is nothing from the apostolic collection, but only no so coherent mass. But in ch. 18 there are again very kindred sayings, which by no means hang closely together among themselves, yet they turn
<!--- page 748 -->
all upon the idea of gradations and differences of worth in the kingdom of heaven; and I think that as such they also formed a separate section in the collection. The introduction here, where Christ has to do with the apostles alone, could not well be much otherwise than we read it in 18:1–2, and the close and transition 19:1 is the usual καὶ ἐγένετο ὅτε ἐτέλεσεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς τοὺς λόγους τούτους, μετῆρεν κ.τ.λ. Chapter 23 does not separate itself in quite so definite a way from what precedes and follows; yet I am inclined to regard it as a special division of the fundamental work, even though the end of the chapter with its content leans somewhat toward what follows. For its introduction does not hang together with what precedes in such a way that it could seem to be of one piece with it. At that time, when no one any longer ventured to ask him, he spoke with the multitude and his disciples; and what follows again has in ἐξελθὼν ἐπορεύετο, καὶ προσῆλθον οἱ μαθηταὶ αὐτοῦ ἐπιδεῖξαι αὐτῷ τὰς οἰκοδομὰς τοῦ ἱεροῦ its own introduction, wholly akin to those hitherto. This section has indeed in content a certain similarity to the anti-Pharisaic material in the Sermon on the Mount; yet the tone and manner of combination feel so different that Matthew, even had he taken no account of times at all in arranging his material, still had reason enough to separate these sayings from those. This comparison, then, of the discipline, so to speak, of his school with the Pharisaic, and the prophecy of the conflict that would develop from this difference, rightly formed a little section of its own. Although it therefore appears divergent that between this section from the collection and the following nothing else
<!--- page 749 -->
is inserted: still I cannot bring myself to regard both as originally one, and to ascribe to the apostle that 24:1 which is so similar to the other introductory formulas of our ἑρμηνευτής. That now chs. 24 and 25 were thus put together in the collection everyone must find as natural as it is improbable that Christ should have spoken this consecutively. Of the parables in this section the same holds, in relation to those in ch. 13, as was said of ch. 18 in comparison with ch. 5. The end is marked as always, for the transition from this discourse-section also to the following narratives is made quite in the accustomed way by καὶ ἐγένετο ὅτε ἐτέλεσεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς τοὺς λόγους τούτους; so that in fact one may assert that wherever this formula—strange in itself, but from this easily explained—occurs in our evangelist, it also marks the end of a section of the apostolic collection.

If we can now without hesitation refer these nine chapters of our Gospel κατὰ Ματθαῖον to the apostle who compiled the discourses of Christ, and yet wish, apart from our Gospel, to picture his work as a whole: we must confess that he would have had to pass over a great deal, and indeed matter wholly of the same kind as what here lies before us, and thus plainly lying in his way, in the sayings of Christ, if he had wished to compile only such as needed only a common heading—like Parables, or Against the Pharisees, or Rules for the Apostles, etc.—and to exclude all for whose understanding some knowledge of the occasioning circumstances was required. If these were communicated as briefly as possible, without any painting, solely for the sake of the discourse: then the book remained wholly true to its character,
<!--- page 750 -->
and could still without further addition be most aptly described by τὰ λόγια συνεγράψατο. Of this kind there is now much in our Gospel, but it is no longer so easy to determine what of it may belong to that source and what our evangelist may have drawn from other special sources or from common tradition. For since such discourses as were proper answers or rejoinders, even in the apostolic collection, if they were joined by a common heading, yet by the nature of the case stood more isolated: the evangelist too could more easily find occasion to scatter them and insert other matter between. Hence here any separation is very precarious, and I will follow only a single indication. The formula ch. 22:46, καὶ οὐδεὶς ἐδύνατο ἀποκριθῆναι αὐτῷ λόγον, οὐδὲ ἐτόλμησέν τις ἀπ᾽ ἐκείνης τῆς ἡμέρας ἐπερωτῆσαι αὐτὸν οὐκέτι, in connection with the following τότε ὁ Ἰησοῦς ἐλάλησεν κ.τ.λ., has so much kinship with the transition-formulas of the elaborator hitherto pointed out, and so easily turns into: And it came to pass, when Jesus had finished answering them all, he spoke to the people and his disciples—that the conjecture almost irresistibly forces itself that here ends such a section from the Matthean collection which contained answers of Christ. Now going backwards from the indicated place we find such first in 22:35–45. The connection in v. 34 is here too only a not very happy transition-formula; for that Christ had silenced the Sadducees could be no special ground for the Pharisees to tempt him, but ought rather to have disposed them favourably toward him. Further, question and answer 22:15–22. Both passages show so great an analogy in treatment that in the apostolic σύγγραμμα
<!--- page 751 -->
they very naturally found their place side by side. The real beginning of this section seems however to be 21:23; and I believe that the second parable 22:1 followed immediately after the first, as it closes in 21:44, and only the evangelist, in order to make the sequence cohere more smoothly, inserted vv. 45–46. Yet of course 20:20–28 also is an answer to a question, and 19:27–20:16 likewise, and then still further back 19:16–26 and 19:3–12. So that all these pieces could have belonged to the same division of the collection. The reason for interrupting a section of the collection contrary to his usual habit, and inserting other matter between, would then for the evangelist lie in the hint that the question 21:23 related to Christ’s intercourse in the Temple, on account of which he had gradually to bring Christ to Jerusalem. And in fact the hint 19:1, μετῆρεν ἀπὸ τῆς Γαλιλαίας κ.τ.λ., and all that he has inserted from other sources from here on—19:13–15; 20:17–19 and 30–34; finally 21:1–22—has plainly this direction. But if anyone prefers to reckon ch. 18 already to this section, so as not to have to regard it as a special one: I should not know much to object. From this whole body of the Gospel, which embraces Christ’s public life and teaching, there would thus at first remain only chs. 8 and 9, further chs. 11 and 12, unless indeed ch. 11 also belongs to the collection. For it likewise contains almost nothing but discourse of Christ in four different paragraphs that follow one another unconnected: first exposition of the difference between the Baptist and himself, then on the people’s behaviour toward both, threat against those among whom he labours in vain, and a new general invitation with attestation of his accord with the divine order. Just so I should
<!--- page 752 -->
also like to vindicate to the collection the first part of ch. 15 and the beginning of ch. 16: yet these passages stand out too little and are too closely bound up with what is more narrative for anything to be asserted of them with equal certainty. And how easily into a composition like our Gospel much could come from other sources that might very well have had a place in the collection, yet was not taken from it and did not stand in it. But it seemed advisable to me to go to this limit, that it might appear the more clearly how the certainty of judgement diminishes when the character of the material and the manner of its insertion no longer give the same indication. What now remains from that main part of the Gospel are such elements of evangelistic tradition as some healings, the feeding, the transfiguration, the entry, which could not be wanting in any connected relation such as the author wished to give. Yet with this we have for the present nothing to do. Outside this main part, however—before ch. 5 and after ch. 25—I do not believe we are to expect constituents of our apostolic collection. In the table-discourses ch. 26:17–35 there is too much that is dialogical, and still less could the passion narrative, however many glorious sayings of Christ occur in it, find a place in a writing that wished only to compile discourses of Christ; rather this could very fittingly close with Christ’s last discourses concerning his return.

Moreover I by no means assert that our Gospel has taken up the whole of the apostle’s collection into itself. Rather this seems to me the less probable. For it would be too wonderful if all the individual parts had lent themselves equally well to such treatment as has here taken place—too wonderful if
<!--- page 753 -->
preference for some parts of the whole had not also done harm to others. Hence other similar elaborators may well have taken up other matter[^753a] and omitted some of what we find in ours. For there seem to have been several who treated the apostolic collection in the same manner as our Gospel, so that one must regard the name εὐαγγέλιον κατὰ Ματθαῖον to a certain extent as a family name. We have reports of two such, namely the εὐαγγέλιον καθ᾽ Ἑβραίους and the Gospel used by the Ebionites. Indeed I should say at least of two; for among the mentions that remain to us there probably still lurk several recensions differing from one another, notwithstanding the one passage of Jerome where he also regards these two as one.[^753b] For since he nowhere else mentions the special Gospel of the Ebionites: they seem to have come in here only by kinship through oversight, whereas the distinction found in Epiphanius is too definite to be overlooked. For he calls the Gospel used by the Nazarenes a most complete Gospel of Matthew, but that of the Ebionites a falsified and mutilated one;[^753c] and he can here have applied no other standard
<!--- page 754 -->
than the canonical Matthew. If then both stand in opposite relations to this: they cannot have been the same. Now I will not indeed assert that the expression πληρέστατον is meant in comparison with the canonical Matthew, as if the Nazarene Matthew had been more complete than ours; but only introduced beforehand as a contrast to the Ebionite, or with reference to the Cerinthians previously treated, who likewise had an incomplete Matthew; yet even so the difference between the Nazarene and the Ebionite would always remain, even if the former, only in Hebrew language, was exactly our Gospel of Matthew. Nor can the mutilation by any means be referred exclusively to the genealogy; for Epiphanius is uncertain about the Nazarenes whether they cut off the genealogies, and yet calls their Gospel the complete one. He also says definitely of the Cerinthians that they used the genealogies, and we should then still have two Gospels of Matthew mutilated in different ways. But neither can I understand this as meaning that they had our present Matthew before them and from it omitted this and that at will. Against that the cited falsifications speak too definitely, some of which are of such a kind that one can regard them only as originally different narratives of the same event;[^754a] others are such that we could assign them no place at all in our Matthew.[^754b] Indeed I should maintain
<!--- page 755 -->
that from what Epiphanius communicates to us from this Gospel one might conclude with fair certainty that, if it had any unity of character and style at all, the narrative part must have been almost throughout divergent from our Matthew. Since now the beginning too, as one clearly sees, was wanting: how did people come to call it a Matthew, if there was not at least as thoroughgoing a similarity between the two as the difference must plainly have run through the whole? And this similarity will then be found in nothing else than in the common foundation of both, the apostolic collection of discourses, by which a similarity of arrangement was already implied even in the narratives inserted between. Against this no one will object on the ground that in these Gospels there stood not only narratives but also individual sayings of Christ foreign to our Matthew. For on the one hand these may also have occurred precisely in narratives; on the other it is most natural that of the apostolic collection itself copies arose which were enlarged by glosses from the common source of evangelistic tradition, sometimes more, sometimes less appropriately and authentically. Yet I am not only compelled to regard the Nazarene and the Ebionite Gospel as different not only from our Matthew but also from each other; but also, when I take together all the relevant passages of Jerome, I cannot convince myself that he everywhere speaks of the same composition. Even apart from the scarcely uncorrupted passage[^755a] where he himself presents that Hebrew book, of which he knew
<!--- page 756 -->
that it was at Caesarea, and which he obtained at Beroea for copying, as the original of our canonical Matthew—whereas otherwise[^756a] he expresses this only as a widely spread opinion—it is hard to conceive how he did not refute more openly and strongly this notion, which from that exemplar which he copied and—for this will surely have been the same—from which he made his Greek and Latin translation of the Gospel καθ᾽ Ἑβραίους must have appeared to him wholly inadmissible and groundless, but rather repeats it several times under various formulas, precisely while citing passages that stand out as wholly foreign from our Matthew. I know how to explain this only either by his having really in secret held that Gospel for the original work of the apostle, and the canonical Matthew for a reworking rather than a proper translation—whence he had even translated that one into Greek, in order to bring it into circulation also among Greek-speaking Christians beside the canonical. Or else, besides that which he translated, he must have known still other exemplars under the same title which stood significantly nearer our Matthew, yet it did not lie in his way to ascertain exactly the relation between the two, and therefore on the one side he likewise presented, not without a certain tacit assent, the opinion which identifies the Gospel of the Hebrews with our Matthew, while on the other he made it his special business to communicate passages from which the great difference between the two must leap to everyone’s eyes. Both explain it somewhat; both also have their great
<!--- page 757 -->
improbabilities; but I see no third, and these ill-harmonizing statements always need an explanation. Only this much seems to me established: that several Gospel-books, notwithstanding very considerable differences, were yet so related to one another that they were all referred back to Matthew; that some[^757a] of these existed in Aramaic and therefore also bore the common name καθ᾽ Ἑβραίους; that the apostle Matthew composed none of all these, but only in Aramaic that collection of discourses and sayings of Christ which Papias ascribes to him; that this lay at the foundation of that whole family of Gospel-books; that of our canonical Gospel of Matthew too the real author is unknown, and it cannot be proved that it was likewise originally composed in Aramaic; finally that Papias knew writings from this family and included them under the formula ἡρμήνευσε δ᾽ αὐτὰ ὡς ἠδύνατο ἕκαστος.

Just as little, however, do I think we need hesitate to assert that Matthew’s writing exercised no similar influence upon the remaining canonical Gospels. For of John nothing need be said; but the author of our Luke, of whom we do not even know whether he was acquainted with Aramaic, if he knew the discourse-collection, yet plainly did not use it, and could not
<!--- page 758 -->
use it, since he found many sayings of Christ which Matthew already in his first section had torn from their historical connection and united with similar ones, in his materials in their real connection. His relation to our Gospel of Matthew, however, especially in the more narrative parts, is a question not belonging here at all. Nearer our subject it would be to inquire whether the parables and other discourses of Christ occurring in Luke, which our Gospel of Matthew does not know, were only passed over by the author of the latter, or whether and why they were also wanting in the apostle’s collection. As for Mark, finally: here the testimony of our Papias leads us wholly away from Matthew to another source.

This testimony is now full enough, but no less difficult, and partly for that reason, partly also because people are almost always accustomed to consider our Gospel of Mark only in relation to the other two, it has been less regarded than it seems to me to deserve. Papias namely treats of a writing of a Mark, whose origin he describes fairly exactly, and of which he speaks with such interest as he would scarcely have done had he not known it himself. But whether this writing is our present Gospel of Mark is to me at least in the highest degree doubtful; and if the question must be answered in the negative, then arises the second question, how our Gospel relates to that writing—a question in answering which we shall scarcely get as far as was possible with Matthew. The origin of the writing he describes as follows. A Mark—whether the New Testament mentions only one of this name or several, and whether ours is this one or
<!--- page 759 -->
one of these, may for the present be quite indifferent—but one of this name, says Papias, was the ἑρμηνευτής of Peter; and here I indeed have no hesitation in rendering the word by “interpreter.” For it follows expressly afterwards that he accompanied Peter, and so ἑρμηνευτής is to be understood as befits this. Peter did not need an explainer or commentator where he himself was, and scarcely can any other relation be thought of than that for the sake of language he needed one who made him intelligible, and indeed no other language than Greek—of which Peter need not have been wholly ignorant to need nonetheless someone who interpreted what he knew how to express fluently only in his mother tongue, and who helped him when somewhere an expression failed him. Now Peter, as the need of his instructions required, also cited in them discourses and deeds of Christ; and whatever of this Mark had retained in memory, that he wrote down accurately—ἀκριβῶς ἔγραψε, says Papias, οὐ μέντοι τάξει.[^759a] The sense of these last words must especially be established; but I believe, when everything is taken together, they cannot possibly mean anything other than merely isolated, without connection.[^759b] In itself considered it could indeed also mean “not in the proper order,” and then the sense would be that Mark had
<!--- page 760 -->
often missed the right chronological order or failed to hold fast a topical order he had begun. But what Peter—or rather the πρεσβύτερος from whose mouth he reports—says in excuse of Mark plainly leads to the former, as also does the whole connection of the discourse. Peter can in his instructional addresses, which he arranged according to need, and in which a historical compilation of the Lord’s discourses was not aimed at at all,[^760a] cite from Christ’s life always only individual items as example or for proof; and if now Mark had no other source, how should he even come upon the thought of making from such fragments a connected history? Precisely this total lack of another source for his record is here most definitely presupposed, and only to ward off every thought of the contrary does the reporter expressly say that Mark never heard Jesus himself, nor ever followed him, but only later Peter.[^760b]

No less clearly does what follows aim at the same. For when it is said that Mark devoted his attention wholly to omitting nothing of what he had heard
<!--- page 761 -->
and falsifying nothing therein:[^761a] he took no thought at all for that other thing—bringing into connection among themselves what he had heard singly and interwoven in the apostle’s instructions. Plainly there also lies in this narrative of Papias that Peter took no further part in Mark’s writing of this work—quite contrary to what later writers, after the usual fashion of pouring still more of their own water into the springs, have also made of this. For all that is said in Mark’s favour could not in the least excuse him for the lack of τάξις, if Peter had had a hand in it. Therefore one must rather believe that Mark wrote down what he had heard only behind Peter’s back, perhaps only when he no longer stood with him in this connection.

What then was this writing of Mark which Papias describes for us? Just as little our Gospel of Mark as the other was our Gospel of Matthew; but likewise a collection—a collection of individual traits from Christ’s life, spoken and done,[^761b] reproduced exactly as Peter had strewn them into his instructional addresses, neither joined into a continuous whole nor arranged in definite sections whether by chronological or by topical order, but written down as they singly emerged in memory.[^761c] And he recorded
<!--- page 762 -->
them probably in Greek, partly because the Aramaic, as the rarer, would expressly have been mentioned, partly also because we must already presuppose, since Mark served the apostle as interpreter, that Greek was the more familiar to him. So then this statement of Papias is indeed, at least immediately, no attestation for our Gospel of Mark; yet it must be worth more to us than if it were only that. For we can now demonstrate what otherwise we should be allowed to set up only as a highly probable conjecture: namely that such collections as these two—collections of discourses and sayings, and collections of individual traits and deeds—naturally preceded connected Gospel-writing after the manner of our Synoptics, because in these times of predominant preaching activity written record found room only in a very limited way, for the sake of need. But not only this: also that the apostolic preaching itself was a chief source for the evangelistic narrative that everywhere accompanied the proclamation of Christianity and, especially where only an indefinite rumour of Christ had first penetrated, essentially supported it, and then gradually also for historiography. If Mark, as is yet most probable, still wrote as Peter’s interpreter: he could also only steal the time for it, as it were, and it would be the most improbable thing in the world that from the materials he caught from Peter he should have made such a writing as our Gospel; rather the most natural is precisely what Papias tells us, that he gladly contented himself
<!--- page 763 -->
with writing down as exactly as possible only what happened to become present to his memory when he gained leisure. Nor did he thereby think of a proper public, of a public publication, but intended, besides wishing to fix what he had heard for himself, at first only communication to those who devoted themselves to the business of evangelists. And for these too the isolated record was the most agreeable; for so each could arrange each item for himself according to his particular manner of presentation and his purposes—as indeed I hope to have shown in our Gospel of Luke such compilations of individual traits of the kind Mark supplied, made according to different points of view. If now someone received these records of Mark with the report that all was from Peter’s mouth, and such a one then wrote a connected history of Christ which however chiefly contained this collection and took up only so much other matter as was necessary: such a writing could with full right be called an εὐαγγέλιον κατὰ Πέτρον. Scarcely has there been ground enough to assert of the writing which existed under this title that it was also related to our Matthew; I should rather assign it the origin just indicated. And the helpers of other apostles probably did the same. If now such collections came into the hands of those who did not properly know how to distinguish genuine and spurious, or who themselves were caught in erroneous opinions about Christ: then under apostolic names Gospels could appear, such as κατὰ Θωμᾶν or κατὰ Βαρθολομαῖον, which the Church had to reject as apocryphal, without its being strictly possible to say that they had been foisted upon those apostles.
<!--- page 764 -->
It will by no means be unexpected to me if those who cannot free themselves from accustomed notions judge of this only that, by means of my own inventions, I have formed a whole romance out of insignificant lines of an unreliable writer: yet it seems to me that in all that has otherwise for a considerable time been said about our Gospels there is more invented than in this presentation; and as for Papias, these statements are not of such a kind that anyone could have intended to impose something upon him concerning them—but his informants could indeed know the truth. If now the question is to be answered whether our Gospel of Mark also attained this name because of its kinship with the writing described by Papias, or wholly independently of this: great caution is indeed needed. If namely it arose from this collection as the Gospel of Matthew from that other: then it could just as well be called an Εὐαγγέλιον κατὰ Μάρκον from the author of the collection as from its warrant and first author an κατὰ Πέτρον. To answer the question it would then first have to be investigated what elements of our Mark can be regarded as capable of having been taken in this way from Peter’s discourses. Without doubt this question is far more difficult than the related one with the Gospel of Matthew; and in only raising it here, since my intention was only to show that Papias’s statement cannot refer immediately to our Gospel, I content myself finally with calling attention only to a couple of points belonging thereto.

First namely the circumstance that our Gospel contains few longer discourses of Christ seems to speak for its origin from Mark’s collection.
<!--- page 765 -->
For discourses spreading over several subjects Peter could not well have communicated in his *Didaskalien* [instructional addresses]; and where we nevertheless find such in our Gospel we should provisionally regard them as added constituents. With the narratives, in which it is far richer, one would have to look first not so much to whether they contain much that concerns Peter’s personality or that he alone could know, but rather only to whether Peter could have used them as models to summon to imitation of Christ, or whether they contain such sayings of Christ by which Peter could have wished to ground his arrangements, prescriptions and teachings. If then in addition the presentation bears that stamp—not indeed easy to describe, yet with some practice very easy to distinguish—by which we recognize what is reported by an ear- and eyewitness: such narratives would be quite especially to be assigned to Mark’s original collection. And if it then appeared that the connection of such pieces with others also lacks the vividness and certainty by which John’s Gospel in this respect so wholly distinguishes itself from the others: this would then furnish still a new proof that the Gospel is in fact composed in this way. Whereas, on the other hand, the more everything seemed of one cast, the sooner we should be compelled to take refuge in the opposite presupposition, that the writing described by Papias has nothing at all to do with our Gospel of Mark, and that the identity of name is only one of those strange accidents to which we should otherwise owe it alone that Eusebius preserved for us that passage of Papias. Yet critical feeling is upon
<!--- page 766 -->
such subjects still so little unanimously developed among us that easily, though acknowledging the same rule, one will come to this result and another to the opposite. Only this I should like still to premise, that the investigation may be conducted the more unprejudicedly: namely that the question which is everywhere wont to be regarded as the most important in this matter—how namely the Gospel of Mark relates to the other two—has no connection whatever with it. For in no case can the author of our Mark have been in exclusive possession of that collection of Peter’s interpreter. Be he the first who used it for a complete Gospel: our Gospel of Matthew may have drawn supplementary material from this, but just as well it may independently have come upon elements of the collection lying at its foundation. And just so the author of our Luke may already count the author of our Mark among those who before him have attempted to set forth a narrative of the Christian events, without perhaps having learned anything of his immediate source; but it is also possible that in his research after the original sources he came upon the collection made from Peter’s discourses without knowing our Gospel of Mark. Our Gospel of Mark may also have been written later than the other two; the author may have been acquainted with these and have supplemented himself from them, without perhaps attending to how these related to his source, and yet, notwithstanding that he always laid this source as foundation, the appearance may have arisen as if he had only followed now the one, now the other of those two. And so it appears that even if one starts from the presupposition that to our Gospel of Matthew
<!--- page 767 -->
there underlies the discourse-collection of the apostle Matthew, to our Gospel of Mark the historical collection of the interpreter Mark, and that both received their name from this relation, nevertheless between our three Gospels in their present state all the different relations are possible which people without that presupposition have claimed to discover. But since now Papias’s statement makes us acquainted with two original writings: this is plainly the first investigation, how the present writings of the same names relate to those. If then it succeeds with some certainty to separate out in our two Gospels what belongs to their peculiar sources: then comparative investigation can begin, with the particular aim of ascertaining whether and how far our Matthew has drawn supplementary material from the source of our Mark and conversely, or both from the evangelistic tradition in general chiefly represented by our Luke. Then finally the confrontation of these with John must show why those almost entirely lack what this communicates to us. In any case, however, Papias’s report, if we accept the presupposition to which it so naturally leads, shows us all the more clearly how excellently our evangelistic treasure is organized. For on the one side John’s writing gives us the most important from the connected reminiscences of one who stood in the closest personal relation to Christ; on the other, Luke’s the results of a scrutinizing research after the most reliable of what concerning Christ was deposited in the common memory, so to speak, of the apostolic Church; and in the middle between these two then stand the two Gospels whose kernel
<!--- page 768 -->
consists of collected individual apostolic reminiscences, which however could become a whole only through supplementation from the total field of the tradition.
